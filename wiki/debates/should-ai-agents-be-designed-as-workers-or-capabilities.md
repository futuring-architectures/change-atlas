# Should AI agents be designed as workers or capabilities?

## Central question

Does treating an AI agent as a co-worker help organizations assign responsibilities and integrate it into work, or does the metaphor preserve inherited roles and import human attributes that prevent system redesign?

## Position A

The worker metaphor is operationally useful. It gives teams a familiar unit for delegation, supervision, access, budgets, and performance expectations. Human-centered language may make unfamiliar systems easier to discuss and constrain.

## Position B

An agent has no career, consciousness, fatigue, or intrinsic motivation. Calling it a worker makes the role the unit of adoption, focusing attention on staffing questions while leaving workflows, decision rights, and production logic unchanged. Capabilities should be allocated across a redesigned system instead.

## Additional positions

- Worker language can be retained as an interface metaphor while architecture and legal accountability explicitly reject personhood.
- Neither framing is sufficient: agents are also products of vendors, data, infrastructure, and governance regimes.
- Human roles carry identity, development, recognition, and rights; capability allocation that ignores these functions can make work more precarious.

## Sources of disagreement

- Whether metaphor is treated as a communication aid or a literal design ontology.
- Whether the unit under redesign is task, role, workflow, capability, or institution.
- How much agent behavior is stable enough to manage as a delegated function.
- Which responsibilities can legally and morally be assigned to software.

## Current assessment

Capability framing is analytically stronger for architecture, but worker language may remain useful at the interface if its limits are explicit. The non-negotiable point is accountability: a metaphor must not allow software to become the bearer of responsibility that belongs to organizations and people.

## Open questions

- Which rights and protections disappear when roles dissolve into capabilities?
- Can a capability model support learning, identity, and career development?
- When does a familiar metaphor aid governance, and when does it conceal category error?
