# What should antitrust optimize for in platform markets?

## Central question

When a platform is widely chosen and produces direct consumer benefits while also controlling access, interoperability, or market structure, which form of power and harm should antitrust prioritize?

## Position A: prioritize consumer choice and direct benefit

Large consumer platforms derive substantial power from voluntary adoption, making interventions aimed at constraining billions of individual choices impractical and potentially undesirable: [[anti-monopoly-vs-antitrust-evi-2f5b61db4f37#Claim 1|platform power from voluntary adoption]] and [[anti-monopoly-vs-antitrust-evi-2f5b61db4f37#Claim 2|limits of constraining user choice]]. Aggregators can also benefit consumers by matching them with desired content, supporting an analysis that does not equate harm to incumbent media firms with consumer harm: [[is-the-internet-different-evi-6c29b15157f2#Claim 2|consumer benefits from aggregation]] and [[is-the-internet-different-evi-6c29b15157f2#Claim 3|a consumer-centred antitrust interpretation]].

The Ecosystems collection adds a dynamic-competition version. Benedict Evans argues that major threats often change the field instead of substituting directly for the incumbent: [[market-definitions-and-tech-monopolies-evi-e8ce23aebea6#Claim 1|historical displacement cases]] and [[market-definitions-and-tech-monopolies-evi-e8ce23aebea6#Claim 2|field-changing competition]]. Conrad Bastable goes further, arguing that technology-monopoly rents fund innovation and help create later challengers: [[monetization-and-monopolies-how-the-internet-you-loved-died-evi-c8c8ec84e77d#Claim 1|the proposed monopoly lifecycle]] and [[monetization-and-monopolies-how-the-internet-you-loved-died-evi-c8c8ec84e77d#Claim 2|the non-intervention position]].

## Position B: prioritize contestability and incumbent control

Interoperability can help entrants reach customers, while a stronger incumbent may withdraw openness when it begins to benefit rivals: [[tech-monopolies-and-the-insufficient-necessity-of-interoperability-evi-c8bbfe1c2ca0#Claim 1|entrant incentives for interoperability]] and [[tech-monopolies-and-the-insufficient-necessity-of-interoperability-evi-c8bbfe1c2ca0#Claim 2|incumbent incentives against interoperability]]. The reported Facebook reversal supplies a candidate case of that mechanism: [[tech-monopolies-and-the-insufficient-necessity-of-interoperability-evi-c8bbfe1c2ca0#Claim 3|Facebook's interoperability reversal]]. From this perspective, current consumer choice can coexist with structural barriers to future competition.

Cory Doctorow proposes repurposing Amazon's catalog and recommendation infrastructure to direct purchases toward local merchants: [[view-a-sku-evi-724432fb60cf#Claim 1|the showrooming problem]] and [[view-a-sku-evi-724432fb60cf#Claim 2|the proposed reversal]]. Daniel Joseph adds that platform value can depend on captured user production: [[delivering-people-evi-acaef3d02086#Claim 1|structural user labor]] and [[delivering-people-evi-acaef3d02086#Claim 2|its relationship to platform concentration]]. These positions ask not only whether a platform innovates, but who supplied its productive infrastructure and whether others can redirect it.

## Additional positions

- [[power-as-an-organizing-design-value|Power as an organizing design value]] suggests that power may be embedded before consumer choice is expressed, through the values and resources shaping system design.
- A layered position could protect consumer choice while targeting exclusionary conduct, switching costs, or interoperability restrictions rather than platform use itself.

## Sources of disagreement

- Evidence: direct consumer benefit versus the conditions for durable contestability.
- Definition: whether power means adoption, market position, control of infrastructure, or design-embedded capacity.
- Scope: harm to consumers, competitors, complementors, publishers, or the wider information environment.
- Timeframe: present revealed preference versus future entry and innovation.
- Values: autonomy and convenience versus pluralism and limits on concentrated control.

The original four claims under Position A come from two publications by the same author, Ben Thompson, and should not be treated as independent corroboration. The Ecosystems sources broaden the authorship but remain interpretations and proposals rather than comparative market studies. The Debate maps competing causal frames; it does not establish the empirical balance of innovation, capture, displacement, and durable control.

## Current assessment

The positions are not strict logical contradictions. Consumer benefit and innovation rents do not disprove structural control or captured participant value; structural control does not prove that breaking a firm up will produce a better ecosystem. Dynamic displacement matters, but “a future adjacent challenger may emerge” does not resolve present conduct. Analysis should state the market layer, timeframe, participant group, claimed innovation mechanism, and remedy rather than treating monopoly as uniformly static or self-correcting.

## Open questions

- Which interventions improve contestability without reducing user autonomy or useful aggregation?
- How should consumer benefit be measured when current preference may reinforce future concentration?
- Which interoperability changes were strategic exclusion rather than responses to privacy, security, or product constraints?
- Which monopoly rents measurably fund transferable innovation rather than defense, acquisition, or extraction?
- When can third parties repurpose incumbent infrastructure without reproducing its dependency and control?
