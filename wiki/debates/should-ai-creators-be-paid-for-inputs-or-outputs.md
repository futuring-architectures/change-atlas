# Should AI creators be paid for inputs or outputs?

## Central question

Should compensation flow when copyrighted material is licensed for training, or when a model produces economically valuable output connected to creative work and style?

## Position A

Input licensing uses familiar ownership, contracts, and auditable transactions. Rights holders can refuse or price access before training, without requiring speculative attribution of every generated output.

## Position B

One-time licensing rewards whoever controls an existing catalog but may not finance continued creation. Output-linked compensation could connect recurring model value to the creative ecosystems on which it depends.

## Additional positions

- Collective funds or levies could support creative capacity without tracing each output to an individual input.
- Payment may be due for labor, market substitution, or ecosystem renewal even where causal attribution is diffuse.
- Output compensation can entrench already legible rights holders and intensify surveillance of creative influence.

## Sources of disagreement

- Whether training is use, learning, copying, or transformation.
- Whether model outputs can be causally and legally attributed to specific works.
- Whether the goal is property enforcement, harm compensation, or sustaining future creation.
- Who can negotiate: individual creators, publishers, platforms, collecting societies, or the public.

## Evidence

## Current assessment

The source identifies a real sustainability problem but supplies one side of the institutional design. Output-linked compensation requires evidence that attribution is technically credible, resistant to gaming, inclusive of less-legible creators, and capable of supporting new work rather than only incumbent catalogs.

## Open questions

- What is the unit of attributable output value?
- Can collective mechanisms avoid impossible item-level tracing?
- How should public-domain and commons-based contributions be represented?
