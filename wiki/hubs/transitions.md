# Transitions

## Orientation

This Hub synthesizes **How We Learn → Transitions**. The collection rejects the idea that transition happens when a technology arrives or when people are informed about it. Capability must be integrated into workflows, institutions, social networks, knowledge practices, values, and continuing outcome governance. Those layers move at different speeds and can disagree about what progress means.

## Synthesis

### Transition is integration across different clocks

Technical capability can change faster than workflows, institutions, standards, identity, and public legitimacy. [[pace-layering|Pace layering]] treats these differences as system properties rather than one universal lag, while the [[transition-integration-stack|Transition integration stack]] connects them to explicit human outcomes and revision loops. Slowness can preserve stability or injustice; speed can enable exploration or amplify error.

### Task performance does not settle the fate of work

Automation forecasts often decompose jobs into precise tasks and omit the ambiguous work between them. [[task-boundary-work|Task-boundary work]] includes interpretation, coordination, exception handling, and accountability. It refines the distinction between technical capability and situated competence: a benchmark can succeed without specifying how the whole practice should change.

### Fluency is epistemic and social

Using AI well is not only interface skill. [[epistemic-fluency|Epistemic fluency]] includes questioning, verification, uncertainty, disagreement, and the choice not to delegate. Individual fluency matters, but it cannot substitute for transparent systems, institutional assurance, recourse, or inclusion.

### Resistance is evidence—but not one kind of evidence

Persistent objection may reveal a factual misunderstanding, distributional harm, loss of control, or a conflict over commonality and values. [[is-technology-resistance-an-obstacle-or-design-input|The resistance Debate]] therefore rejects both automatic dismissal and automatic veto. Selective opposition, voluntary experiments, and the power to change or stop a deployment are transition-design questions.

### Cheap production destroys old proxies

When plausible software becomes cheap, the artifact itself stops reliably proving understanding, commitment, or judgment. [[signal-collapse-after-cheap-production|Signal collapse after cheap production]] moves evaluation toward problem knowledge, retention, learning behavior, system coherence, and outcomes. More experiments can improve innovation, but only if selection and learning improve with production.

### Correct activity can still lose its purpose

Systems can remain available, update successfully, and optimize their metrics while drifting from the outcome they should serve. The [[activity-alignment-gap|Activity-alignment gap]] makes purpose testing a continuing governance function. Recommendation loops that project the cultural past forward are one possible example.

## Emerging propositions

- Transitions that coordinate technical, institutional, social, and cultural pace layers may integrate capability more effectively than transitions governed as a single rollout.
- Resistance that persists after factual objections are addressed may reveal a value or legitimacy conflict that technical correction cannot resolve.
- As implementation becomes cheap, evaluators may need to replace artifact-based proxies with evidence of understanding, judgment, retention, and system outcomes.

These remain explicit synthesis-backlog propositions for future Evidence.
