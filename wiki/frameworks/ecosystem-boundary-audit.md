# Ecosystem boundary audit

## Purpose

Use this Framework when a product, service, or organization depends on substantial activity beyond its employment and ownership boundary. It replaces a single inside/outside line with six auditable maps and prevents “ecosystem” from functioning as a vague synonym for partners or community.

## Structure

### 1. Contribution map

Who supplies labor, content, data, demand, trust, distribution, capital, standards, maintenance, knowledge, or complements? Include unpaid and informal contributions as well as contracted inputs.

### 2. Dependency map

Which contributions are necessary, replaceable, time-sensitive, or concentrated? Trace second-order dependencies: a small complementor may itself depend on one marketplace, cloud, payment rail, model sponsor, or specialist cohort.

### 3. Coordination map

How are actions aligned across the boundary? Record contracts, prices, protocols, standards, APIs, narratives, reputation, shared identity, platform rules, and informal relationships. Note who bears the cost of translation and exception handling.

### 4. Control map

Who can change interfaces, terms, objectives, access, ranking, prices, data use, and technical direction? Distinguish access to an artifact from control over its evolution and infrastructure.

### 5. Capture and risk map

Who receives revenue, ownership, learning, reputation, optionality, and bargaining power? Who absorbs unpaid work, volatility, liability, lock-in, moderation, maintenance, and failure?

### 6. Rights and recourse map

Which participants have information, voice, compensation, portability, appeal, ownership, fork, or exit? Connect each right to a contribution or dependency and test whether it remains usable under conflict.

The six maps are an Atlas synthesis across the collection. No single source proposes this exact structure.

## How to apply it

1. Name the bounded outcome and the formal organization at its center.
2. Draw each map separately before combining them.
3. Mark every critical dependency without meaningful voice or fallback.
4. Identify where contribution and capture diverge.
5. Test a plausible withdrawal, term change, or failure at each concentrated node.
6. Specify the smallest governance, contract, standard, or architecture change that improves alignment.
7. Record who might lose coordination capacity, protection, or investment incentive from that change.

## Worked example

In the fantasy-miniature case, independent artists contribute intellectual property; small manufacturers contribute equipment and production skill; marketplaces provide discovery; hardware and board makers provide technical infrastructure; and financial services coordinate recurring multinational payments. Control and capture sit at several layers. A complete strategy cannot describe the artist and manufacturer relationship while ignoring marketplace discovery, board concentration, payment access, or the recourse available when a complement changes terms.

## Applicability and limitations

- The audit diagnoses distribution; it does not prescribe equal ownership or decentralized control at every layer.
- More formal rights can increase coordination cost and still be justified by dependence or risk.
- Informal relationships can be resilient but difficult to audit and enforce.
- The six maps describe a current system; ecosystems evolve, and a low-risk complement can become infrastructure.
- External law, labor classification, competition policy, and public infrastructure may determine rights beyond the focal firm's design.
