# Network outcome design stack

## Purpose

Use this Framework to avoid explaining a network outcome with a single label such as “social media,” “the algorithm,” or “decentralization.” It traces an outcome through six interacting design layers and asks where intervention would actually change the system.

## Structure

### 1. Connection structure

Who can connect to whom? Inspect density, direction, hubs, clusters, boundaries, portability, and the difference between a social graph and an interest graph.

### 2. Participation and identity

Who can enter, speak, create, rate, transact, fork, or leave? Identify which participant actions are required for the network to function and whether identity is persistent, contextual, portable, or token-weighted.

### 3. Interaction grammar

What can participants do with one another and with content? Examine post length, reply, remix, share, vote, transact, collaborate, and the friction or cadence attached to each action.

### 4. Distribution and feedback

How does an item travel, and which feedback changes its reach? Distinguish requested delivery, social propagation, search, curation, recommendation, and amplification. Record the objective being optimized.

### 5. Economics and ownership

Who pays, who is paid, who owns, and which behavior the revenue model rewards? Include advertising, subscription, transaction fees, tokens, unpaid participant labor, and value capture.

### 6. Governance and recourse

Who sets rules, moderates, changes the protocol, handles disputes, and can be held accountable? Test whether formal decentralization produces usable local agency, viable exit, and cross-context coordination.

The six-layer stack is an Atlas operationalization. The source establishes that topology, connection shape, monetization, product features, communication modes, and algorithmic amplification all matter; the exact grouping and diagnostic questions are synthesized across the Networks Evidence.

## How to apply it

1. State the bounded outcome to explain without naming a cause.
2. Describe the current design at each of the six layers.
3. Mark where the layers reinforce one another and where they conflict.
4. Separate observed behavior from intended behavior and claimed mechanism.
5. Propose the smallest layer change that could alter the outcome.
6. Trace second-order effects through the other five layers.
7. Define what evidence would distinguish improvement from displacement of harm.

## Worked example

For TikTok’s recommendation loop, connection structure is less central than interest-based distribution; viewing generates dense participation signals; short video and rapid swiping define the interaction grammar; ranking uses feedback to allocate attention; advertising rewards sustained engagement; platform governance controls the objective and recourse. The stack prevents “the algorithm” from hiding the interaction and business layers that supply its inputs and goals.

This example is based on a bounded 2022 report and is not a complete audit of TikTok.

## Applicability and limitations

- The Framework is diagnostic, not a causal model; the same layer can produce different outcomes in different populations and periods.
- Layers interact, so changing one may shift behavior into another rather than resolve the problem.
- Some network outcomes arise from external law, culture, devices, or institutions not owned by the network.
- The Framework does not supply a universal definition of “healthy,” “prosocial,” or “decentralized”; those values must be made explicit.
