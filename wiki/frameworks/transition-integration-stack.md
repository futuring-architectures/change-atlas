# Transition integration stack

## Purpose

Use this Framework when a technical capability is becoming possible faster than a social system can absorb it. It treats transition as continuing integration across layers, not as a launch event or an adoption campaign.

## Structure

### 1. Desired human outcome

State whose flourishing, agency, safety, and public value the transition should advance. Make conflicts between stakeholder outcomes explicit before capability becomes the default purpose.

### 2. Technical capability and ingredient

Describe what the technology can and cannot do, under which conditions, and what complementary data, infrastructure, interfaces, and expertise it requires. Separate a demonstration from a deployable system.

### 3. Workflow and task boundaries

Map the whole practice, including interpretation, coordination, exceptions, specification, and accountability between benchmarkable tasks. Decide where work moves rather than inferring job replacement from task performance.

### 4. Institutional pace layers

Identify the layers that can experiment quickly and those that change slowly because they stabilize standards, rights, trust, infrastructure, or identity. Design interfaces and reversible probes between them instead of demanding one transition speed.

### 5. Epistemic fluency and inclusion

Build the ability to question, guide, verify, disagree with, and decline the system. Pair individual fluency with institutional assurance, recourse, accessibility, and protection from coerced participation.

### 6. Cultural legitimacy and network shaping

Treat persistent resistance, social influence, and cultural meaning as transition inputs. Determine whether objections arise from factual uncertainty, distributional harm, loss of agency, identity, or incompatible values, then change the design or the deployment accordingly.

### 7. Activity–alignment monitoring

Monitor whether the operating system still advances its purpose, not merely whether it remains active or technically correct. Revisit goals and measures as context changes; include stop, rollback, and redesign conditions.

## How to apply it

1. Choose one consequential practice and name the desired outcome.
2. Convene affected people, domain experts, operators, and technology owners.
3. Write one testable claim and one failure condition for each layer.
4. Run a bounded probe whose technical and social effects can be observed.
5. Compare capability, workflow, legitimacy, learning, and outcome evidence.
6. Integrate, revise, slow, or stop the transition; then repeat the stack.

## Worked example

For AI-assisted radiology, the capability layer measures bounded image tasks. The workflow layer maps discussion with clinicians, ambiguous cases, patient context, and accountability. Pace layers include model updates, hospital procurement, professional standards, and regulation. Epistemic fluency covers when clinicians can interrogate or override results. The legitimacy layer includes consent, labor effects, and public trust. Alignment monitoring asks whether diagnosis and patient outcomes improve—not only whether the model is used.

## Applicability and limitations

- The layers are coupled and need not be completed in a linear order.
- Slow integration can be prudent or obstructionist; the Framework does not assume either.
- Participation does not eliminate power differences or value conflict.
- A local pilot may miss long-run capacity loss and system-wide redistribution.
- The stack makes tensions inspectable; it cannot calculate one objectively correct transition.
