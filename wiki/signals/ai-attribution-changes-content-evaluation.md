# AI attribution changes content evaluation

## Observation

Ethan Mollick reports that research participants evaluate content differently after learning it was generated by AI than when they assume it came from a person. He interprets this attribution effect as one reason people may conceal AI assistance: perceived authorship changes the reception of otherwise human-like output.

## Why it matters

Evaluation does not depend on output alone. Provenance, identity, expectations, and disclosure rules become part of the cultural selection mechanism. This complicates benchmarks that treat a generated artifact as separable from how it was produced and represented.

## Evidence

- [[detecting-the-secret-cyborgs-evi-945c979b066d#Claim 1|Detecting the Secret Cyborgs — claim 1]] supplies the concealment incentive.
- [[detecting-the-secret-cyborgs-evi-945c979b066d#Claim 2|claim 2]] reports the attribution-dependent judgment effect.

## Limits

- The Evidence summarizes research without naming the studies, effect sizes, or content domains.
- Different evaluation does not necessarily mean worse evaluation, and disclosure can change as norms mature.
- Concealment may create ethical, contractual, and trust costs not measured in the immediate reception effect.
