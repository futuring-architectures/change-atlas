# AI automation remains constrained by deployment economics

## Observation

A January 2024 report on an MIT CSAIL study found that most jobs previously labeled exposed to computer-vision automation were not economically attractive to automate at then-current development and operating costs. Technical task capability therefore overstated near-term substitution.

## Scope and date

- Study report: 2024-01-22.
- Scope: visual-analysis tasks in U.S. non-farm employment under modeled deployment costs and employer incentives.
- Reported estimate: only a minority of wages attached to the studied vision tasks were economically attractive to automate.

## Evidence

- [[ai-wont-steal-as-many-jobs-as-expected-evi-1db89aca324b#Claim 1|AI won’t steal as many jobs as expected — claim 1]] records the study finding.
- [[ai-wont-steal-as-many-jobs-as-expected-evi-1db89aca324b#Claim 2|AI won’t steal as many jobs as expected — claim 2]] interprets the gap between exposure and economic viability.

## Limitations and alternative readings

- The study covers computer vision, not the full range of generative-language or agentic work.
- It models substitution and does not capture every augmentation benefit, strategic motive, or newly created task.
- Falling deployment costs, reusable platforms, and broader models can change the result.

## Interpretation

Organization design cannot move directly from “a model can do a task” to “the task will be automated.” The decision also depends on integration cost, task share, reliability, maintenance, and the value of retained human flexibility.
