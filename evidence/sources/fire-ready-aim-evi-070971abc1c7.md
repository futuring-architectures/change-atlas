## Curated excerpt

> Ready, Aim, Fire made sense when taking a shot was expensive. The first attempt could take years, cost millions, or put lives at risk. Thinking first was rational. AI is changing the cost of the first serious attempt. In more and more work, making a version is becoming the fastest way to figure out what you should make.

## Atomic claims

### Claim 1

The author argues that AI is reducing the cost and time of a serious first attempt, so making a concrete version is increasingly becoming a faster way to discover what should be made than specifying it fully in advance.

- Classification: `conceptual_claim`
- Scope: AI-assisted knowledge work, product development, workflows, and creative production where usable prototypes can be generated cheaply.
- Date relevance: Practitioner claim published 2026-09-01.
- Qualification: “Increasingly” is not quantified, and generation cost is only one part of a serious attempt; context assembly, integration, evaluation, deployment, maintenance, user exposure, and consequence may remain expensive.

### Claim 2

Using an artillery analogy, the source distinguishes early attempts that buy information from later attempts that spend earned confidence and proposes that the appropriate sequence of readiness, aiming, and action follows the economics of being wrong.

- Classification: `conceptual_claim`
- Scope: Decision sequences under uncertainty when attempts differ in cost, reversibility, information value, and consequence.
- Date relevance: Decision model articulated 2026-09-01.
- Qualification: The analogy can make action sound more bounded and observable than social, organizational, or software experiments are; affected people may bear costs even when the initiating team treats an attempt as cheap.

### Claim 3

The article reports that the Wright brothers responded to a 1901 glider producing about one-third of predicted lift by building a small wind tunnel, testing roughly 100–200 wing models, and using the results to develop a much better 1902 glider.

- Classification: `observation`
- Scope: A historical aviation-development case used to illustrate lower-cost experimentation.
- Date relevance: Events in 1901–1902, retold in a 2026 article.
- Qualification: The source provides no citation, exact test count, design records, or comparison of the wind tunnel with other contributors to improvement; the case illustrates a mechanism rather than measuring its general effectiveness.

### Claim 4

The author generalizes wind tunnels, flight simulators, staging environments, prototypes, models, and simulations as deliberately cheaper places for reality to correct a belief before a finished or consequential action is taken, and positions AI as a general-purpose addition to this lineage.

- Classification: `conceptual_claim`
- Scope: Experimental representations and protected environments across engineering, software, science, and knowledge work.
- Date relevance: Cross-domain interpretation published 2026-09-01.
- Qualification: Models and prototypes can provide fast but systematically misleading feedback when they omit relevant users, physical constraints, incentives, dependencies, or rare failures; cheap internal correction does not guarantee external validity.

### Claim 5

The article recommends acting earlier when a first attempt is cheap, reversible, contained, and informative, while retaining more preparation and a higher evidence bar for decisions that are slow, irreversible, or put people at risk.

- Classification: `normative_position`
- Scope: Choosing between advance planning, bounded probes, staged action, and high-consequence deployment.
- Date relevance: Decision rule proposed 2026-09-01.
- Qualification: Teams can underestimate lock-in, data persistence, reputational effects, environmental cost, or involuntary exposure. Reversibility and blast radius require evidence and affected-party review rather than assertion by the experimenter.

### Claim 6

The author interprets many company approval and planning processes as habits designed for an older, higher execution-cost structure and argues that AI exposes the mismatch by producing concrete artifacts while teams are still debating abstractions.

- Classification: `interpretation`
- Scope: Organizational product, workflow, and agent-development decisions that can be prototyped within hours or days.
- Date relevance: Contemporary organizational interpretation published 2026-09-01.
- Qualification: The source supplies no company sample or process evidence. Some approvals protect security, rights, compatibility, budgets, coordination, or safety and remain necessary even when artifact generation is cheap.

### Claim 7

The source argues that a working artifact can participate in thinking by making disagreement specific, revealing unnecessary requirements, and exposing new ideas; it proposes “fire first” when producing the artifact costs less than obtaining enough knowledge to specify it correctly.

- Classification: `normative_position`
- Scope: Exploratory product, software, workflow, and creative work where a prototype can be inspected without consequential deployment.
- Date relevance: Practice recommendation published 2026-09-01.
- Qualification: A prototype can anchor discussion on the first generated form, conceal absent requirements, and reward what is easy to render. The rule needs competing variants, explicit uncertainties, representative users, and stopping conditions to avoid confusing concreteness with truth.

### Claim 8

The article reports a validation bottleneck in which scientific AI agents can generate hypotheses and candidate solutions faster than scientists can physically test them, and argues that analogous gaps are appearing between code generation and team confidence and between research production and decision-maker absorption.

- Classification: `observation`
- Scope: A secondary account attributed to Google DeepMind plus the author's interpretation of software and research work.
- Date relevance: Claimed contemporary pattern as of 2026-09-01.
- Qualification: No DeepMind publication, generation-to-validation ratio, organizational cases, throughput series, or confidence measure is supplied. The source establishes a plausible constraint pattern, not its prevalence or status as the dominant bottleneck.

### Claim 9

As drafts become cheap, the author argues that selection, evaluation, measurement, and expertise become a larger share of work because people must distinguish meaningful evidence from random or impressive-looking output.

- Classification: `conceptual_claim`
- Scope: AI-enabled science, software, research, and organizational decision-making.
- Date relevance: Bottleneck-shift claim published 2026-09-01.
- Qualification: Evaluation may itself be partly automated, expertise can carry blind spots, and the relative cost of judgment is not measured. Cheap generation can also make verification cheaper when tests and feedback are machine-readable.

### Claim 10

The source interprets planning as both legitimate risk management and a possible refuge from falsification, arguing that fast tools do not remove organizational discomfort with discovering that an articulate hypothesis is wrong.

- Classification: `interpretation`
- Scope: Teams choosing between documents, meetings, prototypes, and encounters with observable results.
- Date relevance: Organizational interpretation published 2026-09-01.
- Qualification: Delay can reflect incentives, power, coordination, legal duties, safety obligations, or scarce evaluation capacity rather than psychological avoidance; rapid prototyping can also become performative activity that avoids harder commitments.

### Claim 11

The article warns that cheap attempts create a failure mode of excessive generation and experimentation without learning, and proposes measuring useful speed as the time from an important uncertainty to a trustworthy change in belief rather than the volume of artifacts produced.

- Classification: `conceptual_claim`
- Scope: Organizations using AI to generate prototypes, experiments, research, and decision alternatives.
- Date relevance: Measurement proposal published 2026-09-01.
- Qualification: “Trustworthy change in belief” requires an operational record of priors, evidence quality, affected outcomes, decision change, and appropriate observation windows; belief updates can still be incorrect or strategically reported.

### Claim 12

When real-world action is too consequential, the author recommends manufacturing a cheaper experimental shot whose blast radius is small and information radius is large, so it can be discarded while making the next attempt better.

- Classification: `normative_position`
- Scope: High-uncertainty work that can use sandboxes, simulations, developmental hardware, models, or other staged substitutes before consequential exposure.
- Date relevance: Experimental-design rule proposed 2026-09-01.
- Qualification: A low-fidelity substitute can create false confidence, and a nominally small blast radius may still externalize harms, consume resources, persist data, or exclude affected parties from defining what information matters.

### Claim 13

The source argues that exploration should stop once evidence has earned sufficient conviction: cheap first attempts are valuable because they support later commitment, not because adjustment and generation should continue indefinitely.

- Classification: `normative_position`
- Scope: The transition from exploratory iteration to consequential organizational, product, scientific, or creative action.
- Date relevance: Stopping and commitment rule proposed 2026-09-01.
- Qualification: The article does not define sufficient evidence, account for disagreement over who decides, or specify how sunk cost, strategic urgency, changing conditions, and minority risk should affect the commitment threshold.
