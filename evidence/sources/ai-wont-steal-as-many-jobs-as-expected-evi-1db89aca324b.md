> *Contrary to what one might expect, the MIT researchers found that the majority of jobs previously identified as being at risk of AI displacement aren’t, in fact, “economically beneficial” to automate — at least at present \[…\] The key takeaway is that the coming AI disruption might happen slower — and less dramatically — than some commentators are suggesting.*

## Atomic claims

### Claim 1

An MIT CSAIL study found that most jobs previously identified as exposed to computer-vision automation were not economically attractive to automate at then-current costs.

- Classification: `observation`
- Scope: U.S. non-farm jobs containing visual-analysis tasks modeled in the cited 2024 study.
- Date relevance: Study report published 2024-01-22.
- Qualification: The study examined computer-vision tasks rather than generative-language work, modeled costs and employer incentives, and did not cover augmentation or newly created tasks.

### Claim 2

AI-related labor disruption may unfold more slowly and less dramatically than task-exposure estimates imply because technical feasibility does not equal economic viability.

- Classification: `interpretation`
- Scope: Near-term automation of visual tasks under the study's cost assumptions.
- Date relevance: Interpretation published 2024-01-22.
- Qualification: Deployment costs can fall, general-purpose models can broaden scope, and organizational or strategic motives may lead firms to automate before narrow cost parity.
