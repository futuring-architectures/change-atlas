## Curated excerpt

> Even among those who do use AI, the skill gap is enormous. Users of AI who have no idea what they’re doing are far worse off after AI than they were before […] Using AI well is a *craft*. It takes enormous iteration to get really good at using AI, and the inertia of someone who isn’t on AI is insurmountable for many. Turning a slop-cannon into a refined power user is just as hard.

## Atomic claims

### Claim 1

The author reports a recurring enterprise AI-usage barbell in which roughly 5–10% of workers become power users, about 20% use the tool weakly, and about 70% barely use it; in one operations organization, the rollout registered as adoption without increasing team speed.

- Classification: `observation`
- Scope: Founder-reported conversations and implementations in organizations ranging from roughly 50 to 5,000 people, including one non-technical enterprise operations organization.
- Date relevance: Contemporary enterprise AI-rollout account published 2026-08-07.
- Qualification: The pattern, categories, baseline, and outcome are self-reported without sampling, records, time series, or independent verification; the author sells enterprise agent implementation services.

### Claim 2

The source argues that binary adoption measures such as login or prompt counts conceal a spectrum of user competence and can coexist with little improvement in workflow outcomes.

- Classification: `conceptual_claim`
- Scope: Enterprise AI adoption dashboards and rollout reporting.
- Date relevance: Measurement critique published 2026-08-07.
- Qualification: Usage can be a useful leading or diagnostic indicator when paired with quality, task, cost, and outcome measures; the source does not test alternative measurement systems.

### Claim 3

Proficient AI use is presented as a learned craft that includes context management, converting repeated instructions into reusable system guidance, assigning judgment to models selectively, using deterministic code elsewhere, and inspecting outputs before acceptance.

- Classification: `conceptual_claim`
- Scope: AI-assisted knowledge work, illustrated through software engineering.
- Date relevance: Practitioner account of AI-use competence in August 2026.
- Qualification: The listed practices are domain- and tool-specific examples rather than a validated general competence model, and some may be automated or displaced by later systems.

### Claim 4

The author reports another enterprise commitment in which approximately 10% of licensed users consumed 90% of AI tokens, making nominal seat availability a poor description of actual use intensity and cost exposure.

- Classification: `observation`
- Scope: One unnamed enterprise licensing commitment described as eight figures annually.
- Date relevance: Contemporary enterprise account published 2026-08-07.
- Qualification: The source supplies no measurement window, user count, token-price schedule, task mix, value produced, or independent confirmation.

### Claim 5

The author predicts that at least half of an organization will not become proficient interactive AI users and that successive capability releases may widen the gap by raising the skill floor for effective use.

- Classification: `prediction`
- Scope: Enterprise employees using rapidly changing general-purpose AI tools.
- Date relevance: Forward-looking claim published 2026-08-07.
- Qualification: The forecast universalizes current consulting impressions and does not compare interface simplification, peer learning, role redesign, accessibility, education, or generational change.

### Claim 6

The source interprets vendor attention to frontier users and power users' private productivity advantage as incentives that keep the organizational skill gap open.

- Classification: `interpretation`
- Scope: Enterprise AI product development and internal knowledge sharing.
- Date relevance: Incentive interpretation published 2026-08-07.
- Qualification: Power users can also be intrinsically motivated to teach, standardize, reduce risk, or improve collective outcomes; neither vendor-roadmap influence nor deliberate knowledge withholding is measured.

### Claim 7

The author recommends treating training as a diagnostic for identifying interested and capable users, then rewarding power users for publishing reusable skills in a shared, ranked repository.

- Classification: `normative_position`
- Scope: Enterprise AI enablement and internal knowledge diffusion.
- Date relevance: Rollout recommendation published 2026-08-07.
- Qualification: Ranking can reward visibility over safety or reuse, repositories require maintenance and governance, and the source reports no controlled comparison or durable adoption outcome.

### Claim 8

The source recommends a two-path implementation: support the power-user minority in interactive use while embedding agents into existing systems of record for other workers and involving humans at approval, rejection, editing, or exception points.

- Classification: `normative_position`
- Scope: Repetitive enterprise workflows in finance, sales, procurement, operations, and related functions.
- Date relevance: Organization-design recommendation published 2026-08-07.
- Qualification: Background automation can reduce informed control, hide errors, narrow learning, and turn workers into exception handlers; the proposed segmentation risks fixing current skill differences into durable job architecture.

### Claim 9

The author recommends replacing board-level adoption reporting with measures of how much work is manual, hybrid, or fully automated.

- Classification: `normative_position`
- Scope: Enterprise AI portfolio and workflow reporting.
- Date relevance: Measurement recommendation published 2026-08-07.
- Qualification: Work-mode shares remain activity measures and need outcome, quality, cost, safety, worker-agency, distribution, and counterfactual baselines to establish value.
