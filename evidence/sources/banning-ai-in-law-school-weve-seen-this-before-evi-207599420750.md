## Atomic claims

### Claim 1

Early institutional restrictions on portable computers and word processors at Harvard and Cornell were overtaken within a few years by widespread student and workplace adoption that changed writing, analysis, and teaching practices.

- Classification: `interpretation`
- Scope: The author's historical account of personal-computer adoption in US law school, university, and early-career settings during the 1980s.
- Date relevance: Historical interpretation published 2026-07-11; examples concern roughly 1982–1985.
- Qualification: The account combines personal recollection and cited contemporary reporting but does not compare institutional learning, equity, or educational outcomes under different adoption policies.

### Claim 2

The article characterizes “preemptive regulation” and “presumptive negatives” as incumbent strategies that use fear or moral concern to slow adoption before demonstrated harms outweigh realized benefits.

- Classification: `interpretation`
- Scope: Institutional and regulatory responses to technologies already entering practical use.
- Date relevance: Interpretation published 2026-07-11.
- Qualification: The source argues from selected historical analogies and does not establish that precaution is generally incumbent-serving or that harms must be realized before proportionate safeguards are justified.

### Claim 3

Because students and new professionals are becoming AI-native, the article predicts that categorical attempts to stop their AI use will be ineffective and may resemble earlier failed efforts to contain personal computers and spreadsheets.

- Classification: `prediction`
- Scope: AI adoption in legal education and professional work.
- Date relevance: Prediction published 2026-07-11.
- Qualification: Adoption pressure does not determine which uses institutions can effectively restrict, redesign, or condition, and AI may differ materially from earlier information tools.

### Claim 4

The source argues that using information tools is intrinsically linked to freedom of expression, so controlling access to AI can also control expression.

- Classification: `normative_position`
- Scope: Governance of general-purpose AI use in education and work.
- Date relevance: Position published 2026-07-11.
- Qualification: Expression rights do not settle conflicts involving privacy, intellectual property, safety, professional duties, unequal exposure, or institutional responsibility.

### Claim 5

New information tools can change how a problem is represented and explored rather than merely automate an existing answer, as spreadsheets turned financial work toward multi-variable modeling and simulation.

- Classification: `conceptual_claim`
- Scope: Transformative effects of general-purpose information tools on knowledge work.
- Date relevance: Historical analogy used in an argument published 2026-07-11.
- Qualification: The spreadsheet case is illustrative rather than comparative evidence, and transformed practice can introduce new errors, dependencies, and exclusions.

### Claim 6

The article advocates governing AI harms through existing rules against cheating, theft, injury, discrimination, and other misconduct rather than through categorical technology bans.

- Classification: `normative_position`
- Scope: Institutional and public governance of current AI use.
- Date relevance: Position published 2026-07-11.
- Qualification: Existing law and institutional rules may not cover every AI-specific risk, allocate responsibility clearly, or provide timely detection and recourse.
