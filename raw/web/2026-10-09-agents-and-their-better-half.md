Part 1 - From agentic participants to agentic markets

Over the past few weeks, a new class of solutions is taking shape: a market-facing representative of the consumer.

Muse and Instinct - consumer-side AI agents - can search, evaluate and eventually transact on your behalf.

This seems like the natural endpoint of the agent economy: every consumer gets an agent. Instead of visiting multiple sites, they work through an agent.

It is easy to interpret this through familiar mental models. If consumers increasingly begin their journeys with a single agent, that agent can look like the next super-app or aggregation layer - a new place where demand can be concentrated.

But that framing may be too narrow. Super-apps and aggregators centralize the interface through which consumers access an otherwise familiar market.

Agents have the potential to do something far more transformative: not merely aggregate access to existing offers, but represent consumer intent in a form that can interact directly with agents on the other side of the market.

Markets are not made up of consumers alone. If agents are going to change markets rather than simply change how consumers navigate them, we need to look at what happens on the other side of the transaction.

The distinction is between a new locus of aggregation and a new mechanism of coordination. The former changes where the consumer enters the market. The latter can change the very structure of the market.

This series of posts - Agents, and their better half - explores how we’re at a point where the entire structure of market activity is up for reorganisation.

When agents remain single

The promise of Muse and Instinct isn’t simply better search or evaluation of options - it is primarily representation in the market. The agent knows enough about an individual to act on that individual’s behalf.

The underlying assumption is that giving a market participant a sufficiently capable representative will transform how that participant navigates the market. This is already happening. An agent that understands its principal’s objectives can search more extensively, evaluate more options, and continuously compare alternatives in ways that would be expensive to do manually.

But there is an inherent asymmetry in this view.

Markets are relationships between economic actors, while most agents today represent only one actor in that relationship. Muse may develop a rich understanding of what a consumer wants, but it largely encounters merchants through catalogues, product feeds, websites and APIs. A travel agent may understand a traveller’s preferences in considerable detail, but airlines and hotels still largely expose predetermined inventory, prices, and packages. A procurement agent may become considerably better at specifying a company’s requirements and evaluating suppliers, while suppliers continue to respond through conventional catalogues, quotations and bids.

The result is a lopsided agent economy.

Intelligence is accumulating on one side of many market relationships without an equivalent change in how the other side participates. This is easy to overlook because we tend to evaluate agents individually: how well an agent understands a user, reasons about alternatives or completes a task.

Markets, however, should be evaluated in terms of transactions/interactions. An agent’s capabilities are important, but what is equally important to executing the transaction is what happens when the counterparty to which it directs its intent can understand, reason and respond in kind.

Let’s evaluate this with a few examples.

What changes once the counterparty is represented as well becomes clearer in markets such as hiring, travel and procurement.

These are three verticals that I am analyzing through the Agentic AI Observatory. The following helps us better understand why the supply-side needs to be transformed for agentic participation in order for demand-side or consumer-side agents to truly unlock value.

Hiring

Indeed’s Apply For Me allows an agent to act on behalf of a job seeker, while its Sourcing Assistant acts from the employer side by continuously identifying and engaging candidates. Indeed temporarily paused the automatic application mode of Apply For Me after employer feedback, noting that changing how applications are submitted also required new ways for job seekers and employers to engage with AI.

The underlying issue is not simply application volume - even though that’s how it’s being diagnosed and explained away today.

A candidate-side agent can know that someone is willing to accept a lower title for greater scope, or would relocate only for a sufficiently attractive opportunity, or has adjacent experience that makes an apparently imperfect match attractive. A conventional job description cannot reason about that intent.

Conversely, an employer-side agent can know which requirements are genuinely non-negotiable, whether compensation can stretch for an exceptional candidate, and which characteristics have predicted success in the role.

Once both sides are represented, hiring begins to look less like search against two static documents - a résumé and a job description - and more like interaction between two sets of objectives and constraints.

Indeed is already moving some distance in this direction: its employer-side tools can learn matching criteria and continuously source candidates rather than simply wait for applications. The important shift is that the market can discover matches that neither side would have specified completely in advance.

Travel

Imagine a traveller’s agent that knows that she is willing to leave a day earlier for a nonstop itinerary, strongly values a late hotel checkout, prefers one hotel chain but will switch for a substantially better room, and will spend more if a disruption can be handled flexibly. The agent can continuously search against this richer set of constraints.

But if airlines and hotels remain represented only by published fares, room categories and availability feeds, most of that intelligence cannot be applied. The traveller’s agent can optimize among pre-existing offers, but it cannot negotiate with the supplier to reconsider what they are willing to offer.

Now give the airline or hotel an agent as well. It could reason over inventory, yield targets, loyalty status, expected occupancy, ancillary capacity and the traveller’s expressed flexibility and respond with alternate offers that meet the buyer agent’s needs while meeting the seller’s constraints. The transaction is no longer produced by a buyer agent choosing from a catalogue. It is produced through mutual negotiation between represented parties.

B2B procurement

A procurement agent might know that a manufacturer needs 5,000 units of a component, but also that delivery reliability matters more than the lowest unit price and that a certain technical specification can be relaxed. It can search far more intelligently than a conventional sourcing process.

Yet if suppliers continue to appear only as catalogues, price lists and responses to predefined RFQs, the buyer’s richer representation once again encounters a relatively static counterparty. The procurement agent becomes better at selecting from what suppliers have chosen to expose; the market itself does not become much more adaptive.

A supplier agent changes that.

It could analyze production capacity, inventory, margins, alternative specifications, and the strategic value of the customer. It is representing the supplier’s ability and willingness to adapt.

The Agentic Procurement Observatory analyzes how the two sides of the market are evolving. While there are substantial inroads achieved in agentic buying and solutions have emerged for agentic sales/CRM as well, there is no agentic market activity yet.

We have agentic participants but don’t have agentic markets yet. More on that follows.

Why agents need their better half

With an agent on only one side, intelligence improves navigation of the existing market.

With sufficiently capable representation on both sides, intelligence can begin to change what the parties offer one another.

A one-sided agent primarily improves the allocation of attention and decision-making within the existing market. It can lowers search costs and improve matching and shift bargaining power toward the consumer. But the set of possibilities it evaluates has still largely been determined beforehand by sellers.

In this configuration, the buyer has become adaptive while supply remains largely pre-composed.

Much of what is currently described as agentic commerce therefore represents a significant improvement in market participation without yet constituting a fundamentally different market mechanism.

That changes when the counterparty also acquires an agent. Instead of the buyer’s agent selecting from a set of offers constructed in advance, agents on the two sides can participate in mutually constructing an executable offer.

Better halves and agentic markets

This suggests a useful distinction between agentic participants and agentic markets.

An agentic participant uses intelligence to represent its principal more effectively within an existing market mechanism.

An agentic market begins to emerge when multiple parties can express objectives, constraints and trade-offs through agents, and when their interaction determines not merely which existing offer is selected but what offer is made.

Seen this way, the current agent economy is at a very early stage. We have concentrated first on giving individual market participants increasingly capable representatives. The larger economic transformation begins when those representatives encounter one another.

When agents meet their better halves!
